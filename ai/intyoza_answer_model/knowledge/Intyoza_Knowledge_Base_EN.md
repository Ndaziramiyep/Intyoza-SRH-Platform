# Intyoza Knowledge Base (English)

## Rwanda SRH facts for young people (checked October 2026)

**Laws**
- Law N° 026/2025 regulating healthcare services (in force 18 September 2025): people aged 15 and above can consent to health services, including contraception, HIV prevention and other sexual and reproductive health services, without parental or third-party consent.
- Law N° 68/2018 (Penal Code), defilement: 20 to 25 years in prison; life imprisonment when the child is under 14. Sex between children aged 14 or older without violence or threats is not punished.
- Abortion is legal when the pregnant person is a child, after rape, forced marriage or incest, or when the pregnancy endangers the health of the mother or fetus. No court order is needed (since 2019). A ministerial order published in December 2024 allows authorised health centres and clinics, as well as hospitals, to provide it, usually before 22 weeks. For a minor, if parents and child disagree, the child's wish prevails.
- Legal age of marriage: 21.

**Figures**
- Rwanda DHS 2025: 8% of girls aged 15 to 19 have ever been pregnant, up from about 5% in 2019-20. Total fertility rate 3.7 (2020: 4.1). Antenatal care with at least 4 visits: 78% (2020: 47%). Maternal mortality 149 per 100,000 live births (2020: 203).
- HIV: about 1.2% of young women and 0.5% of young men aged 15 to 24 live with HIV; young people account for around a third of new infections.
- Gender-based violence cases handled rose from 33,844 in 2021 to 42,743 in 2025.
- Legal abortion services: about 3,200 people in fiscal year 2025/2026, up 45.5%.

**Services**
- 48 Isange One Stop Centres, linked to 37 district hospitals and 10 teaching hospitals: free, confidential medical, counselling, police and legal support for GBV and child abuse.
- Youth corners (youth-friendly services) at many health centres and youth centres.
- HPV vaccination for school-age girls (single dose); Gardasil 9 rollout started in 2026 in Karongi, Nyarugenge, Kayonza and Gicumbi.
- HIV self-test kits being expanded to schools, universities and youth centres.

## Menstruation and puberty

### MEN-032: What is puberty?

Puberty is the time when a child's body changes into an adult's body. It usually starts between 8 and 13 for girls and between 9 and 14 for boys. Hormones cause fast growth, hair under the arms and around the genitals, pimples and new feelings. Girls develop breasts and start their periods; boys' voices deepen, their penis and testicles grow, and they may have wet dreams. Everyone starts at their own time, and all of this is normal.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### MEN-033: What is the menstrual cycle, and what is ovulation?

The menstrual cycle is the monthly process that prepares a woman's body for a possible pregnancy. It is counted from the first day of one period to the first day of the next, usually 21 to 35 days. Around the middle of the cycle, an ovary releases an egg: this is ovulation, and the days around it are when pregnancy is most likely. If the egg is not fertilised by sperm, the lining of the uterus comes out as the period.

*risk: None; sources: MEN; PUB*

### MEN-034: What are the reproductive organs?

Reproductive organs are the parts of the body used to make babies. A girl's include the vulva on the outside, and inside the vagina, the uterus (womb) where a baby grows, and two ovaries that hold eggs. A boy's include the penis and the scrotum, which holds two testicles that make sperm from puberty. Knowing the correct names helps you look after your body and explain a problem to a health worker.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### MEN-001: What is a period?

A period is when the uterus sheds blood and tissue through the vagina. It is a natural and healthy part of growing up. It usually lasts 2 to 5 days, but this is different for everyone.

*risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-002: When will I get my first period?

Most girls get their first period between ages 10 and 16. Every body has its own timing. Things like poor nutrition, a lot of hard exercise or some health problems can delay it. If you are worried about your timing, a health worker can check that everything is okay.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-003: My period comes at different times every month. Is something wrong with me?

Cycles are often around 28 days, but they can be shorter or longer, and it is common for them to change from month to month in the first years after periods start. See a health worker if your periods stay very irregular, come very rarely, or last much longer than usual.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-004: My period is so heavy I soak through pads fast and feel dizzy. Is that okay?

Very heavy or long periods are a sign to get checked. They can have causes like a hormone imbalance, and losing a lot of blood can cause anaemia, which makes you feel tired, weak or dizzy. Please visit a health worker soon. If you feel faint or bleeding is very heavy, go today.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: MEN*

### MEN-005: Is it normal to have strong pain during my period?

Cramps during periods are common. But pain should not stop your life. If the pain makes you miss school or daily activities, a health worker can help find the cause and ways to manage it.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-006: Can I bathe or wash during my period?

Yes. Washing your body and private parts with clean water and soap during your period is part of good hygiene. Beliefs that washing during a period is harmful are not true, and avoiding washing can increase the risk of infection.

*risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-007: Can I play sport and go to school when I have my period?

Yes. You can exercise, play sport, swim, bathe, work and go to school during your period. Any limits are cultural, not because your body cannot do it.

*risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-008: Are there foods I should not eat or cook during my period?

No. There are no foods you need to avoid during your period, and you can cook as usual. Eating well actually helps your body.

*risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-009: What can I use during my period? Pads, cloth or something else?

You can use disposable pads, reusable pads, menstrual cups, tampons, period underwear or clean absorbent cloth. Pick what is comfortable and available for you. Change it regularly, change in a clean private place, and wash reusable products with soap and water. If you use tampons, change them often.

*risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-010: Can I get pregnant if I have sex during my period?

Yes, it is possible, especially if your cycles are short or irregular. Also, some bleeding that is not a period can be mistaken for one. The only reliable way to prevent pregnancy is a modern contraceptive method.

*risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-011: When does puberty start?

For girls, puberty can start as early as 8 or as late as 13 or 14. For boys, it can start around 9 or as late as 15. All the changes can take several years. Everybody goes through puberty at their own pace.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### MEN-012: What changes happen to girls in puberty?

Girls usually grow taller quickly, develop breasts, and grow hair under the arms and around the genitals. Some get pimples and stronger body smell. Periods begin. The first period means the body can now become pregnant through unprotected sex.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### MEN-013: What changes happen to boys in puberty?

Boys grow hair on the face, under the arms and around the genitals. They grow taller, shoulders get wider, the voice gets deeper and the genitals grow. Some get pimples or stronger body smell. Erections happen more often, sometimes at random times. Some boys get slight breast growth that goes away.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### MEN-014: I woke up wet at night. What is a wet dream and is it normal?

A wet dream is when semen comes out during sleep. It is a normal part of puberty. Having wet dreams, or not having them, are both normal.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### MEN-015: Why do my moods change so much lately?

Puberty brings changes in feelings too. Mood swings, new romantic or sexual feelings and wanting more privacy are all common. If you feel sad or worried most of the time, talk to someone you trust or a health worker.

*risk: None; sources: PUB; MH*

### MEN-016: How do I keep track of my period?

Mark the first day of each period on a calendar or in a phone app. Count the days from the first day of one period to the first day of the next: that is your cycle. After a few months you will see your pattern, which helps you get ready and notice changes.

*risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-017: Is it normal to have white discharge from my vagina?

Yes. A clear or white discharge without a strong smell is normal and keeps the vagina clean and healthy. It can change during your cycle. See a health worker if it becomes yellow, green or grey, smells bad, itches or burns.

*risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-018: My period is late but I have never had sex. Why?

Periods can be late because of stress, illness, big weight changes, hard exercise or a cycle that is still settling after puberty. If you have never had sex, it is not pregnancy. If periods stop for 3 months or more, see a health worker.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-019: I worry my penis is too small. Is that normal?

Penises come in many sizes and keep growing until the end of puberty, often into the late teens. Size when soft says little about size when erect, and it does not affect being a good partner or having children. If you have pain or other worries, a health worker can check privately.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### MEN-020: Does male circumcision protect against HIV?

Medical male circumcision lowers a man's risk of getting HIV from a female partner by about 60%, and makes hygiene easier. It does not give full protection, so condoms are still needed. It is done safely and for free at many public health facilities in Rwanda.

*Where to go: Health centre or hospital; risk: None; sources: WHO*

### MEN-021: Is masturbation normal?

Yes. Masturbation is common among both boys and girls and is a normal way to explore your body. It does not cause infertility, weakness, madness or harm to your body. It is a personal choice, and it is also fine not to do it.

**For ages 10-14:** Touching your own body is a normal part of growing up and is not harmful. It should be done in private. If anyone else touches your private parts or asks you to touch theirs, tell a trusted adult or call 116.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### MEN-022: I found a lump in my breast. Should I worry?

Breast lumps in teenagers are usually harmless, often linked to hormones and the menstrual cycle. Still, any new lump should be checked by a health worker, especially if it grows, hurts a lot, or there is discharge from the nipple.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: WHO*

### MEN-023: My period started at school and I have no pad. What can I do?

Stay calm, it happens to many girls. Ask a teacher, school nurse or a friend you trust for a pad. Until then, you can fold clean toilet paper or a clean cloth into your underwear. Tie a sweater around your waist if you are worried about stains.

*risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-024: Before my period I feel moody, bloated and tired. Why?

Many girls get premenstrual symptoms a few days before their period, such as mood changes, bloating, sore breasts, tiredness or spots, caused by hormone changes. Sleep, exercise, water and eating regularly help. If symptoms are severe or stop your daily life, see a health worker.

*risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-025: I bleed between my periods. Is that a problem?

Light spotting between periods can happen with a new contraceptive method, around ovulation or in the first years of periods. But bleeding after sex, heavy bleeding, or bleeding with pain or bad-smelling discharge should be checked by a health worker. If you could be pregnant, take a pregnancy test.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: MEN*

### MEN-026: My period pain is so strong that painkillers don't help.

Very strong period pain that stops you from school or work, or is not helped by painkillers, should be checked. It can be caused by conditions like endometriosis, which can be treated. Go to a health centre and describe exactly how the pain affects you.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: MEN; WHO*

### MEN-027: What can I take for period cramps?

A hot water bottle on your belly, gentle exercise and rest often help. Common painkillers from a pharmacy, such as paracetamol or ibuprofen, can reduce cramps; follow the dose on the package or ask the pharmacist. If pain is severe, see a health worker.

*Where to go: Pharmacy or health centre; risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-028: My periods are very irregular and I have a lot of acne and body hair.

Irregular periods together with acne and extra hair on the face or body can be signs of a hormone condition such as PCOS. It is common and can be managed. See a health worker, who can check and advise on treatment.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: WHO*

### MEN-029: Can a girl who has never had sex use a menstrual cup or tampon?

Yes. Using a menstrual cup or tampon does not affect virginity, which is about sexual experience, not the body. Start with a small size, relax, and follow the instructions. If it hurts, stop and try again later or use pads.

*risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-030: How do I throw away used pads?

Wrap the used pad in paper or its wrapper and put it in a covered bin or latrine. Do not flush it in a toilet, as it can block pipes. Wash your hands afterwards. Reusable pads should be washed with soap and dried in the sun.

*risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MEN-031: I feel dizzy and weak during my period.

Feeling a little tired is common, but dizziness and weakness can be signs of low iron (anaemia), especially if periods are heavy. Eat iron-rich foods like beans, green vegetables, eggs and meat, and drink water. If you faint, feel very weak or bleed heavily, see a health worker.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: MEN; WHO*

## Contraception

### CON-037: What is family planning?

Family planning means deciding if and when to have children, and using a method to prevent pregnancy until you are ready. Methods include condoms, pills, the injection, the implant and the IUD. Some last a day, others months or years, and all of them are reversible except sterilisation: you can have children after you stop. Condoms are the only method that also protects against HIV and STIs. A health worker or youth corner can help you choose, confidentially.

*risk: None; sources: FP*

### CON-036: What is a condom?

A condom is a thin cover, usually made of latex rubber, that a man wears on his erect penis during sex. It stops sperm from entering the vagina, so it prevents pregnancy, and it is the only method that also protects against HIV and other STIs. Use a new condom every time, from the start to the end of sex. There is also a female condom that a woman puts inside her vagina. Condoms are free at health centres and youth corners, and sold in pharmacies and shops.

*risk: None; sources: FPH*

### CON-038: What is the emergency pill?

The emergency pill is a pill a woman can take after unprotected sex, or if a condom broke, to prevent pregnancy. It works best the sooner it is taken: within 3 days for most pills, and up to 5 days for some. It is not an abortion pill: it does not end a pregnancy that has already started, and it does not affect future fertility. It is for emergencies, not for regular use. Ask at a health centre, youth corner or pharmacy.

*risk: None; sources: EC*

### CON-001: What ways are there to prevent pregnancy?

There are many options. Implants and IUDs work very well and last for years. Pills, injections, patches and rings work well when used correctly and on time. Condoms prevent pregnancy and also protect against STIs. Counting 'safe days' and pulling out are less reliable. A nurse can help you choose what fits you.

**For ages 10-14:** Pregnancy can only happen after sex. The surest way to avoid it at your age is not to have sex. When you are older, there are safe methods like condoms, pills and implants, and a nurse can explain them. If anyone pressures or forces you into sex, tell a trusted adult or call 116.

**For ages 20-24:** Options include the implant (3 to 5 years), copper IUD (up to 10 to 12 years), injection (every 3 months), daily pill, and condoms, which also prevent STIs. Long-acting methods are the most effective and fertility returns after removal. A nurse can help you choose based on your plans for children, health and preferences.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: FP*

### CON-002: Which family planning method also protects against HIV and STIs?

Condoms are the only method that protects against both pregnancy and STIs, including HIV. Other methods, like pills or implants, prevent pregnancy only. Many people use a condom together with another method.

*risk: None; sources: FP; FPH*

### CON-003: Will using family planning stop me from having children later?

No. Modern contraceptive methods do not cause infertility. With most methods, you can get pregnant soon after stopping. With some, like the injection, it can take a few months.

*risk: None; sources: FP*

### CON-004: How well do condoms prevent pregnancy?

When used correctly every single time, about 2 in 100 women get pregnant in a year. With how people usually use them, sometimes skipping or using them wrong, it is about 15 in 100. Using a condom correctly every time makes the difference.

*risk: None; sources: FPH*

### CON-005: How do I put on a condom correctly?

1. Check the pack is not damaged or expired, and open it with fingers, not teeth. 2. Put it on the tip of the hard penis before any contact, rolled side out. 3. Roll it all the way down. 4. After coming, hold the rim and pull out while still hard. 5. Slide it off without spilling, wrap it and throw it in the bin or latrine. Use a new condom every time.

*risk: None; sources: FPH*

### CON-006: Can we use Vaseline or body lotion with a condom?

No. Oil-based products like Vaseline, lotion, cooking oil, baby oil or butter damage condoms and can make them break. Use water-based or silicone-based lubricant, or water.

*risk: None; sources: FPH*

### CON-007: Is it safer to wear two condoms at once?

No, using two condoms at once is not recommended. The rubbing between them can make them more likely to break. One condom used correctly is the safest.

*risk: None; sources: FPH*

### CON-008: The condom broke during sex. What should I do now?

Act soon. Emergency contraceptive pills can lower the chance of pregnancy and work best the sooner you take them, within 5 days. If there is a chance of HIV, go to a health centre as soon as possible for PEP, ideally within 24 hours and no later than 72 hours. Washing or douching does not help.

*Where to go: Health centre or pharmacy; risk: Low; sources: FPH; EC; PEP*

### CON-009: Can I wash a condom and use it again?

No. A condom is used only once. Use a new one every time you have sex.

*risk: None; sources: FPH*

### CON-010: We had sex without protection. Is it too late for the emergency pill?

Emergency contraception can be used up to 5 days after sex, but it works best the sooner you use it. A copper IUD put in by a health worker within 5 days is the most effective option. Go to a health centre or pharmacy as soon as you can.

**For ages 10-14:** If you had sex, go to a health centre or tell a trusted adult as soon as possible: there is medicine that can prevent pregnancy if taken within 5 days. If someone forced you or is much older than you, that is not your fault: call 116 or go to Isange (3029).

*Where to go: Health centre or pharmacy; risk: Low; sources: EC*

### CON-011: Is the emergency pill the same as an abortion?

No. Emergency pills work by stopping or delaying the release of an egg. They do not cause abortion, and they cannot end a pregnancy that has already started or harm it.

*risk: None; sources: EC*

### CON-012: Is the emergency pill safe for teenagers?

Yes. There is no age limit, and it is safe for girls and women. Some people feel sick, get light bleeding or feel tired, which usually passes quickly. If you vomit within 2 hours of taking it, talk to a pharmacist or nurse because you may need another dose.

*Where to go: Pharmacy or health centre; risk: None; sources: EC*

### CON-013: Is it bad to use the emergency pill many times?

It has no known serious health risks, but using it often can disturb your periods and cause more side effects. It is also less effective than a regular method. A nurse can help you find a regular method that suits you.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: EC*

### CON-014: Will the emergency pill stop me from having babies in future?

No. Emergency contraception does not affect your future fertility.

*risk: None; sources: EC*

### CON-015: Is counting safe days or pulling out enough to prevent pregnancy?

These methods are less reliable than modern methods. Many pregnancies happen with them, especially when cycles are irregular, as they often are for young people. A condom or another modern method protects you much better.

*risk: None; sources: FP; HDI2*

### CON-016: Where can a young person get family planning without being judged?

Youth-friendly corners at health centres are there for young people and can explain all options privately. Since September 2025, Law N° 026/2025 allows people aged 15 and above to get contraception and other sexual and reproductive health services without parental consent. Pharmacies also sell condoms and emergency pills.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: TBC; HDI2*

### CON-017: I feel ashamed to buy condoms. People will think I am a sex worker.

Buying condoms means you care about your health and your partner's. Married couples and many other people use them too. You can also ask at a youth-friendly corner, where staff are there to help, not judge.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: FPHM; HDI1*

### CON-018: How does the implant work and how long does it last?

The implant is one or two small soft rods put under the skin of the upper arm by a health worker. It releases a hormone that prevents pregnancy for 3 to 5 years, depending on the type, and works more than 99% of the time. It can be removed any time, and fertility returns quickly. Changes in bleeding are common and not harmful.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: FP; FPH*

### CON-019: How does the family planning injection work?

The injection contains a hormone that prevents pregnancy for 3 months, so you need a new injection every 3 months on time. It works very well when not late. Periods may become irregular or stop, which is not harmful. After stopping, it can take a few months for fertility to return.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: FP; FPH*

### CON-020: How do I take the family planning pill, and what if I forget one?

Take one pill every day at about the same time. If you forget one, take it as soon as you remember, even if that means two pills in one day, then continue as normal. If you miss two or more pills, use condoms for 7 days and ask a nurse or pharmacist; you may need emergency contraception if you had sex.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; pharmacy; risk: None; sources: FP; FPH*

### CON-021: Can a young woman who has never had a baby use an IUD?

Yes. The copper IUD is safe for young women, including those who have never given birth. A health worker places it in the uterus; it prevents pregnancy for up to 10 to 12 years and can be removed any time. Periods may be heavier at first. It does not protect against STIs.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: FP; FPH*

### CON-022: What is a female condom?

A female condom is a soft pouch put inside the vagina before sex. It protects against both pregnancy and STIs, including HIV, and the woman can control it. Use a new one each time and never use it together with a male condom.

*Where to go: Health centre; pharmacy; risk: None; sources: FPH*

### CON-023: Do contraceptives make you fat or change your periods?

Some methods can change bleeding patterns, for example lighter, irregular or no periods with the implant or injection. Some people notice small weight changes with the injection, but most methods do not cause big weight gain. These changes are not dangerous. If a side effect bothers you, a nurse can help you switch methods.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: FP*

### CON-024: I am 15. Can I get contraception without my parents?

Yes. Under Rwanda's Law N° 026/2025 regulating healthcare services, in force since September 2025, people aged 15 and above can get sexual and reproductive health services, including contraception, without parental consent. A youth-friendly corner at a health centre is a good place to go, and your information should be kept private.

**For ages 10-14:** The law lets people aged 15 and above get these services on their own. If you are younger, you can still go to a health centre: health workers will help you and may involve a trusted adult. If someone is having sex with you, call 116.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: LAW25*

### CON-025: Do I have to pay for family planning?

Public health centres in Rwanda usually offer family planning methods free or at low cost, and condoms are often free there. Pharmacies sell condoms, pills and emergency pills. Ask at your nearest health centre or youth corner what is available.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: FP*

### CON-026: My boyfriend refuses to use a condom. What can I do?

You have the right to protect yourself. Tell him calmly that you will only have sex with a condom, because it protects you both. If he still refuses, you can say no to sex. Pressure to have sex without protection is not respect. You can also use a female condom or another method for pregnancy, but only condoms protect against STIs.

*risk: Low; sources: HDI2; FPHM*

### CON-027: Can breastfeeding prevent pregnancy?

Only in specific conditions: the baby is under 6 months, you breastfeed day and night with no other foods, and your period has not returned. If any of these changes, you can get pregnant, so start another method. A health worker can advise methods that are safe while breastfeeding.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: FPH*

### CON-028: Where can I buy the emergency pill?

Emergency contraceptive pills are sold at pharmacies and are available at health centres and youth corners. You do not need to explain your private life. Take it as soon as possible after unprotected sex, within 5 days.

*Where to go: Pharmacy or health centre; risk: None; sources: EC*

### CON-029: Which family planning method is best for a teenager?

There is no single best method; it depends on what suits you. Long-acting methods like the implant or IUD work best because you don't need to remember anything and they last years. Pills and injections work well if used on time. Condoms are the only method that also protects against STIs, so many young people use a condom plus another method.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: FP; FPH*

### CON-030: Do antibiotics stop the pill from working?

Most common antibiotics do not stop the pill from working. A few medicines, like rifampicin for TB and some epilepsy and HIV medicines, can make pills, the implant and the emergency pill less effective. Always tell the health worker or pharmacist that you use contraception.

*Where to go: Pharmacy or health centre; risk: None; sources: FPH*

### CON-031: Can boys and men use family planning?

Yes. Condoms are the main method for men and protect against STIs too. Vasectomy is a permanent method for men who are sure they want no more children. Men also share responsibility by supporting their partner's choice and going with her to the clinic.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: FPH*

### CON-032: Is it okay to keep a condom in my wallet?

Heat and rubbing can damage condoms, so a wallet or back pocket is not a good place for a long time. Keep them somewhere cool and dry, check the expiry date, and don't use one if the packet is torn or the condom is dry, sticky or brittle.

*risk: None; sources: FPH*

### CON-033: I can't feel my implant in my arm anymore.

If you can't feel your implant, use condoms until a health worker checks it. It may simply be deeper under the skin. The health worker can locate it and confirm it is still working or replace it.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: FPH*

### CON-034: How soon does the implant or injection start working?

If you get it during the first days of your period, it works right away. Otherwise, use condoms or avoid sex for the next 7 days. The copper IUD works immediately. Ask the health worker which applies to you.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: FPH*

### CON-035: How do natural family planning methods work?

Natural methods, like counting days or using cycle beads, mean avoiding sex or using condoms on the days you are most likely to be fertile. They only work well for women with regular cycles of 26 to 32 days and with careful daily tracking. For young people, whose cycles are often irregular, they are much less reliable.

*risk: None; sources: FPH*

## Pregnancy

### PRG-027: How does pregnancy happen?

Pregnancy starts when a man's sperm meets a woman's egg. This usually happens during vaginal sex without protection: sperm swim through the uterus to the fallopian tube, where they can fertilise the egg. The fertilised egg then attaches to the wall of the uterus and grows into a baby over about 9 months. A girl can get pregnant the first time she has sex, and even before her first period, because an egg can be released before the first period comes.

*risk: None; sources: PUB; AP*

### PRG-001: Can I get pregnant the first time I have sex?

Yes. Once a girl has started her periods, she can get pregnant any time she has unprotected sex, including the very first time.

**For ages 10-14:** Yes. Once a girl has started her periods, sex can cause pregnancy, even the first time. At your age it is best to wait. If someone is pressuring or touching you, that is not okay: tell a trusted adult or call 116.

*risk: None; sources: PUB; HDI1*

### PRG-002: How do I know if I am pregnant?

A missed period is often the first sign. Others can include feeling sick, sore breasts and feeling very tired. A pregnancy test at a health centre or pharmacy can tell you for sure.

*Where to go: Health centre or pharmacy; risk: Low; sources: TBC*

### PRG-003: I think I am pregnant and I am so scared. I don't know what to do.

You are not alone, and it is okay to feel scared. A nurse at a youth-friendly corner can confirm if you are pregnant and talk with you privately about your options and next steps. If someone forced you to have sex, there is free help for you right now.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner; Help now if forced; risk: High; sources: AP*

### PRG-004: Is it dangerous to be pregnant as a teenager?

Pregnancy at a young age carries higher health risks, like high blood pressure problems and infections for the mother, and babies born early or small. Going for antenatal care early at a health centre helps keep you and the baby safe.

**For ages 20-24:** Pregnancy in your twenties is generally safer than in the teenage years, but antenatal care is still essential. Start visits in the first 3 months, take iron and folic acid, and plan to give birth at a health facility. Spacing births at least 2 years apart protects you and your children.

*Where to go: Health centre (antenatal care); risk: Low; sources: AP*

### PRG-005: Is abortion legal in Rwanda?

In Rwanda, abortion is legal when the pregnant person is a child, or the pregnancy came from rape, forced marriage or incest, or it puts the health of the mother or baby at risk. No court order is needed. Since a 2024 ministerial order, it can be provided at authorised health centres and clinics as well as hospitals, usually before 22 weeks. For a minor, parents usually make the request, but if they disagree with the child, the child's wish prevails. Your information must be kept confidential.

*Where to go: Recognised doctor at a licensed hospital or polyclinic; risk: Low; sources: LAW; CRR*

### PRG-006: A friend gave me pills to end a pregnancy. Is it safe to take them?

Please do not take pills or herbs from someone who is not a health worker. Unsafe abortion is a serious danger to your health and life. Talk to a doctor or nurse first so you know your options and stay safe.

*Where to go: Health centre or hospital; risk: High; sources: AP*

### PRG-007: An older man forced me and now I am pregnant.

This is not your fault, and you deserve care and safety. Isange One Stop Centres give free medical, psychological and legal help: call 3029. If you are under 18 you can also call 116. Pregnancy from rape is one of the legal grounds for abortion in Rwanda, and a doctor can explain your options.

*Where to go: Isange One Stop Centre 3029; 116; risk: Emergency; sources: UNHCR; RNP; LAW*

### PRG-008: I just had a baby. Where can I get support as a young mother?

Health centres offer care for you and your baby after birth, including family planning when you are ready. Connecting with other young parents can also help. Look for the Young parents group in Community.

*Where to go: Health centre; Community group; risk: None; sources: AP*

### PRG-009: When should I start antenatal care visits?

Start as soon as you know you are pregnant, ideally in the first 3 months, and attend all scheduled visits. Antenatal care checks you and the baby, tests for HIV and syphilis, gives vitamins and vaccines, and helps you plan a safe birth at a health facility.

*Where to go: Health centre (antenatal care); risk: Low; sources: AP; DHS25*

### PRG-010: What are danger signs during pregnancy?

Go to a health facility immediately if you have bleeding from the vagina, severe headache or blurred vision, swelling of the face or hands, fever, strong belly pain, fits, water leaking, or the baby stops moving. These can be serious. Call 112 or go straight to the nearest health centre or hospital.

*Where to go: Go to a health facility now; 112; risk: Low; sources: AP; WHO*

### PRG-011: I am pregnant. Can I continue school?

Yes. In Rwanda, pregnant girls and young mothers can continue their studies and return to school after giving birth. Talk to a teacher or school leader you trust, and go for antenatal care. Your education still matters and your future is still yours.

*Where to go: School; youth-friendly corner; risk: Low; sources: AP*

### PRG-012: I think I am having a miscarriage. What should I do?

Bleeding with cramps in early pregnancy can be a miscarriage, and you need to be checked today. Go to a health centre or hospital now, especially if bleeding is heavy, you have fever or feel faint. A miscarriage is not your fault, and care and support are available.

*Where to go: Health centre or hospital today; risk: High; sources: AP; WHO*

### PRG-013: How can I tell my parents I am pregnant?

It is normal to feel afraid. Choose a calm moment, or ask a trusted adult like an aunt, teacher or health worker to be with you or to help start the conversation. Parents are often shocked at first but many become supportive. If you fear violence or being thrown out, a health worker or Isange (3029) can help you stay safe.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner; risk: Low; sources: AP*

### PRG-014: My girlfriend is pregnant. What should I do as the father?

Stay calm and support her. Go with her to the health centre for a pregnancy test and antenatal care, listen to her feelings, and talk about the next steps together. Being a responsible father means supporting her and the child. If she is under 18 and you are an adult, this is a serious legal matter.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner; risk: None; sources: AP*

### PRG-015: Can I get pregnant without full penetration?

Yes, it is possible. Fluid that comes out before ejaculation can contain sperm, and sperm near the opening of the vagina can move inside. The risk is lower than with full sex but not zero. Use a condom or another method, and emergency contraception if you are worried.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### PRG-016: Can oral or anal sex cause pregnancy?

Oral and anal sex do not cause pregnancy, unless semen gets near the vagina. But they can pass STIs, including HIV, so condoms are still important.

*risk: None; sources: STI*

### PRG-017: What should I eat when I am pregnant?

Eat a variety of foods every day: beans and other proteins, vegetables, fruits, grains, and milk or eggs if possible. Take the iron and folic acid tablets given at antenatal care. Drink clean water, and avoid alcohol and smoking.

*Where to go: Health centre (antenatal care); risk: None; sources: AP; WHO*

### PRG-018: Can I drink alcohol or smoke while pregnant?

No. Alcohol and smoking can harm the baby's growth and brain and increase the risk of miscarriage and early birth. There is no safe amount of alcohol in pregnancy. If you find it hard to stop, a health worker can help.

*Where to go: Health centre (antenatal care); risk: None; sources: AP; WHO*

### PRG-019: I feel sick and tired in early pregnancy. Is that normal?

Yes. Nausea, vomiting, tiredness, sore breasts and needing to pee more are common in the first months. Eat small meals often and rest. If you cannot keep any food or water down, lose weight or feel very weak, go to a health centre.

*risk: None; sources: AP*

### PRG-020: Why should I give birth at a health facility?

Health workers can handle problems that can happen suddenly during birth, like heavy bleeding or a baby who struggles to breathe. Giving birth at a health centre or hospital is safer for you and your baby. Plan ahead how you will get there when labour starts.

*Where to go: Health centre or hospital; risk: None; sources: AP; DHS25*

### PRG-021: Since my baby was born I feel very sad and can't cope.

Many new mothers feel low for a few days after birth, but sadness that lasts more than two weeks, or makes it hard to care for yourself or your baby, may be postnatal depression. It is common and treatable. Talk to a health worker or call 114. If you think of harming yourself or your baby, get help immediately: call 112.

*Where to go: Health centre; 114; risk: Low; sources: MH*

### PRG-022: How long should I breastfeed my baby?

Give only breast milk for the first 6 months, with no water or other foods, then add other foods and continue breastfeeding up to 2 years or beyond. Breast milk protects the baby from illness. A health worker can help if breastfeeding is painful or difficult.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: WHO*

### PRG-023: After giving birth, when can I get pregnant again?

You can get pregnant again before your period returns, sometimes within weeks after birth if not breastfeeding fully. Waiting at least 2 years before the next pregnancy is healthier for you and the baby. Ask about a family planning method soon after birth; many are safe while breastfeeding.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: FPH*

### PRG-024: My pregnancy test is negative but my period hasn't come.

A test done too early can be negative even if you are pregnant. Wait a week and test again, using first morning urine. If it is still negative and your period hasn't come, stress, weight changes or hormones may be the cause; see a health worker if it continues.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: AP*

### PRG-025: I am pregnant and have strong pain on one side of my belly.

Strong pain on one side of the lower belly in early pregnancy, especially with bleeding, dizziness or shoulder pain, can be an ectopic pregnancy, which is a medical emergency. Go to the nearest hospital immediately or call 112.

*Where to go: Hospital now; 112; risk: Emergency; sources: WHO*

### PRG-026: I am worried I can't have children. When should I see a doctor?

Most young people can have children. If you have been trying to get pregnant with regular sex and no contraception for 12 months, both partners should see a health worker. Untreated STIs are a common cause of infertility, so testing and treatment protect your future fertility.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: WHO*

## STIs and HIV

### HIV-019: What is HIV, and what is AIDS?

HIV is a virus that weakens the body's defence system (immunity) against infections. AIDS is the late stage of HIV infection, when the body has become very weak and gets serious illnesses. HIV passes through unprotected sex, infected blood, and from mother to baby; not through hugging, sharing food or mosquitoes. There is no cure yet, but daily treatment (ART) keeps people healthy and stops HIV from becoming AIDS, and when the virus is undetectable it is not passed on through sex. Testing is free at health centres.

*risk: None; sources: STI*

### STI-001: What are STIs and how do people get them?

STIs are infections passed mainly through sex, including vaginal, anal and oral sex. Some can also pass from a mother to her baby during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding, or through unsafe blood.

**For ages 10-14:** STIs are infections that pass from one person to another mostly during sex. You cannot get them from hugging, sharing food or toilets. Learning about them now helps you stay safe later. If you have questions, ask a trusted adult or a health worker.

*risk: None; sources: STI*

### STI-002: Can I have an STI and not know it?

Yes. Most STIs have no symptoms at all. The only way to know is to get tested.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: STI*

### STI-003: My private parts itch and there is a smelly discharge. Is it an STI?

These can be signs of an infection, which may or may not be an STI. Please visit a health worker to get checked and treated. Avoid sex or use a condom until you have been seen.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: STI; HDI1*

### STI-004: Can STIs be cured?

Many can. Chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis and trichomoniasis can usually be cured with antibiotics from a health worker. Herpes and HIV cannot be cured, but medicine controls them well. Hepatitis B can be treated to protect the liver.

*risk: None; sources: STI*

### STI-005: Do condoms protect against all STIs?

Condoms protect very well against most STIs, including HIV, when used correctly every time. They protect less against infections that cause sores on skin the condom does not cover, like herpes or syphilis.

*risk: None; sources: STI*

### STI-006: Are there vaccines against STIs?

Yes. Safe vaccines exist for HPV, which can cause cervical cancer, and for hepatitis B. Ask at your health centre or school health programme about getting vaccinated.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: STI*

### STI-007: I was treated for an STI. Does my partner need treatment too?

Yes. Your partner should also be tested and treated. Otherwise the infection can come back to you or spread to others.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: STI*

### STI-008: What happens if an STI is not treated?

Untreated STIs can cause serious problems, like infections that lead to infertility in women, some cancers from HPV, and a higher chance of getting HIV. Getting tested and treated early prevents this.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: STI*

### STI-009: Can I just buy antibiotics myself for an STI?

It is better to see a health worker. Some STIs, like gonorrhoea, no longer respond to many antibiotics, so you need the right test and the right treatment.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: STI*

### HIV-001: How do people get HIV?

HIV passes mainly through sex without a condom. It can also pass from a mother to her baby during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding, and through unsafe blood. Condoms protect very well against HIV.

**For ages 10-14:** HIV passes through sex without a condom, through blood, and from a mother to her baby. You cannot get it from playing, hugging, sharing food or mosquito bites. People with HIV can live long, healthy lives with medicine.

*risk: None; sources: STI*

### HIV-002: Can you tell if someone has HIV by looking at them?

No. Many people with HIV look and feel healthy. A test is the only way to know.

*risk: None; sources: STI; HDI1*

### HIV-003: Is there a cure for HIV?

Not yet. HIV cannot be cured, but daily treatment controls it, and people with HIV can live long, healthy lives.

*risk: None; sources: STI; HDI1*

### HIV-004: Where can I get tested for HIV?

Health centres offer HIV testing, including rapid tests that give results quickly. Your result is private. Youth-friendly corners are a good place to start.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: STI*

### HIV-005: I had sex without a condom last night with someone who might have HIV. What do I do?

Go to a health centre today. There is medicine called PEP that can stop HIV after exposure. Start it as soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours and no later than 72 hours. You can also ask about emergency contraception.

**For ages 10-14:** Tell a trusted adult and go to a health centre today: there is medicine called PEP that can prevent HIV if started within 72 hours. If someone forced you, it is not your fault: go to Isange (3029) or call 116.

*Where to go: Health centre today; risk: High; sources: PEP; EC*

### HIV-006: What is PrEP?

PrEP is medicine for people who do not have HIV, to lower their chance of getting it. It comes as daily pills and also as a ring or injections. It is for people at higher risk. A health worker can tell you if it is right for you.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: PREP*

### HIV-020: What is PEP?

PEP (post-exposure prophylaxis) is HIV medicine taken after a possible exposure to HIV, for example sex without a condom, a condom that broke, or rape. It must be started as soon as possible and within 72 hours (3 days); the sooner, the better. It is taken every day for 28 days. PEP is free at health centres, hospitals and Isange One Stop Centres. If you think you were exposed, go today.

*risk: None; sources: PEP*

### HIV-007: My partner has HIV and takes treatment. Can I still get it?

When someone with HIV takes treatment and their viral load becomes undetectable, they do not pass HIV to sexual partners. Regular check-ups confirm this. Talk with a health worker together about staying protected.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: UEU*

### STI-010: What is the HPV vaccine and who gets it in Rwanda?

HPV is a common virus spread through sexual contact that can cause cervical cancer later in life. Rwanda vaccinates school-age girls against HPV, now with a single dose, and in 2026 began rolling out Gardasil 9, which protects against more types. The vaccine works best before sexual activity starts. Ask at school or your health centre.

*Where to go: School health programme; health centre; risk: None; sources: HPV26; STI*

### STI-011: I have painful sores on my private parts. What could it be?

Painful sores or blisters can be caused by infections like herpes or syphilis. Go to a health worker soon to get checked and treated, and avoid sex until then. Many causes can be treated, and herpes can be controlled with medicine.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: STI*

### STI-012: I have itching and thick white discharge. Is it an STI?

Itching with thick white discharge is often a yeast infection (thrush), which is common and usually not sexually transmitted. It is easily treated, so see a health worker or pharmacist. Avoid douching and tight synthetic underwear. If it keeps coming back or smells bad, get checked for other infections.

*Where to go: Health centre or pharmacy; risk: Low; sources: WHO*

### STI-013: It burns when I pee and I need to pee often. What is it?

This is often a urinary tract infection, which is common in girls and women and needs treatment from a health worker. Drink plenty of water. If you also have discharge, sores or recently had unprotected sex, it could be an STI, so get tested. Go quickly if you have fever or back pain.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: WHO*

### STI-014: How is hepatitis B spread and can I be vaccinated?

Hepatitis B is a virus that attacks the liver. It spreads through blood, sex without a condom, and from mother to baby at birth. A safe vaccine protects you for life, and babies in Rwanda are vaccinated. Ask at a health centre about testing and vaccination if you were not vaccinated.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: STI; WHO*

### STI-015: When should women start cervical cancer screening?

Women are generally advised to start cervical cancer screening from age 30, or from 25 if living with HIV, and repeat it as advised by a health worker. Screening finds changes early, when they are easy to treat. It is quick and available at many health facilities in Rwanda.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: WHO*

### HIV-008: Can I test myself for HIV at home?

Yes. HIV self-test kits let you test privately using a mouth swab or a drop of blood, and Rwanda is making them more available in health facilities, schools, universities and youth centres. A positive self-test must be confirmed at a health centre. A negative result may not show infections from the last 3 months.

*Where to go: Health centre; pharmacy; youth centres; risk: None; sources: HIVY*

### HIV-009: I tested negative right after risky sex. Am I safe?

Not yet certain. Tests can miss a new HIV infection in the first weeks, called the window period. Test again about 3 months after the risk. If the risky sex was within the last 72 hours, go now for PEP. Use condoms until you are sure.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: WHO*

### HIV-010: Can people living with HIV have relationships and children?

Yes. With daily treatment, people living with HIV can have healthy relationships, sex and children. When the virus is undetectable, they do not pass HIV to partners, and with care during pregnancy and breastfeeding, babies are almost always born free of HIV.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: UEU; WHO*

### HIV-011: Can a pregnant woman with HIV protect her baby?

Yes. If she takes HIV treatment during pregnancy, birth and breastfeeding, and the baby gets preventive medicine and testing, the chance of passing HIV to the baby becomes very small. That is why HIV testing is part of antenatal care.

*Where to go: Health centre (antenatal care); risk: None; sources: WHO*

### HIV-012: I am living with HIV. How do I tell my partner?

Telling a partner is a personal step and you can take time to prepare. A counsellor at your health centre can help you plan what to say, or tell them with you. Choose a private, calm moment. If you fear violence, talk to the counsellor or Isange (3029) first.

*Where to go: Health centre; counsellor; risk: None; sources: WHO*

### HIV-013: How common is HIV among young people in Rwanda?

Most young people in Rwanda do not have HIV, but new infections among youth remain a concern. About 1.2% of young women and 0.5% of young men aged 15 to 24 are living with HIV, and young people account for around a third of new infections. Condoms, testing, PrEP and PEP help protect you.

*risk: None; sources: HIVY*

### STI-016: What are the signs of gonorrhoea or chlamydia?

They often have no signs, especially in girls. When signs appear, they include burning when peeing, unusual discharge from the vagina or penis, pain in the lower belly, or bleeding after sex. Both are cured with the right antibiotics from a health worker, and partners must be treated too.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: STI*

### STI-017: What is syphilis and how do I know if I have it?

Syphilis is an STI that often starts as a painless sore on the genitals or mouth, then can cause a rash on the hands and feet. The signs can disappear while the infection stays and harms the body over time. A simple blood test finds it, and it is cured with antibiotics.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: STI*

### STI-018: I have small bumps like warts on my private parts.

Small, painless bumps can be genital warts, caused by HPV. They are not dangerous but can spread through skin contact during sex. A health worker can confirm and treat them. The HPV vaccine helps prevent them.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: STI; HPV26*

### STI-019: There is a fishy smell from my vagina, especially after sex.

A thin grey or white discharge with a fishy smell is often bacterial vaginosis, a common imbalance that is not usually an STI. It is treated with medicine from a health worker. Avoid washing inside the vagina, as that makes it worse.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: WHO*

### STI-020: What happens when I go for an STI test?

A health worker will ask a few private questions, may look at the area, and may take a urine sample, a swab or a small blood sample. It is quick and confidential. Many STIs can be treated the same day.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: STI*

### STI-021: Can I get an STI from oral sex?

Yes. Oral sex can pass infections like herpes, gonorrhoea, syphilis and HPV, though the risk of HIV is low. Using a condom or a barrier reduces the risk.

*risk: None; sources: STI*

### STI-022: Can I get an STI from a toilet seat or sharing towels?

STIs are passed mainly through sex, not toilet seats, swimming pools or sharing cups. A few, like pubic lice, can rarely pass through shared towels or bedding, so it is good hygiene not to share them.

*risk: None; sources: STI*

### STI-023: My private parts itch badly at night and I see tiny insects.

This may be pubic lice or scabies, which spread through close contact. They are treated with lotions or medicine from a pharmacy or health centre. Wash your clothes and bedding in hot water, and your partner should be treated too.

*Where to go: Health centre or pharmacy; risk: Low; sources: STI*

### HIV-014: What are the first signs of HIV?

Many people have no signs at first. Some get flu-like symptoms such as fever, sore throat, rash or swollen glands 2 to 4 weeks after infection, which then go away. You can't tell from symptoms; the only way to know is an HIV test.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: WHO*

### HIV-015: Can I get HIV from kissing, hugging or sharing food?

No. HIV does not pass through kissing, hugging, shaking hands, sharing food, cups or toilets, or mosquito bites. It passes through blood, sex without a condom, and from mother to baby.

*risk: None; sources: WHO*

### HIV-016: I am a young person living with HIV and tired of taking pills every day.

Many young people feel this way, and your feelings are valid. Taking treatment every day keeps you healthy and keeps the virus undetectable so you don't pass it on. Talk to your health worker about side effects, reminders, or joining a peer support group of young people living with HIV.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: WHO; UEU*

### HIV-017: How long do I take PEP and does it have side effects?

PEP is taken every day for 28 days, without missing doses. Some people feel tired, sick or have stomach upset at first; these usually improve. Don't stop early. Go back for an HIV test after finishing as advised.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: PEP*

### HIV-018: Can I get HIV from razors, tattoos or piercing?

Yes, if the equipment has someone else's blood on it. Never share razors or needles, and only get tattoos or piercings where new, sterile needles are used.

*risk: None; sources: WHO*

## Consent and relationships

### REL-001: What does consent mean?

Consent means a free and clear yes to each sexual act. Anyone can change their mind at any time. Silence, pressure, threats, fear or being drunk is not a yes.

**For ages 10-14:** Consent means your body belongs to you. Nobody, not even someone you know or love, should touch you in a way you don't want. You can always say no, and if someone touches you in a bad way or asks you to keep a secret about it, tell a trusted adult or call 116.

*risk: None; sources: TBC; HDI2*

### REL-002: My boyfriend says if I love him I will have sex with him.

You never have to prove love with sex. Someone who loves you will respect your choice and wait until you are ready, or accept if you never want to. Pressure is not love.

**For ages 10-14:** Love is never proven with sex, and at your age it is best to wait. If someone, especially an older person, pressures you, tell a trusted adult or call 116.

*risk: Low; sources: HDI2*

### REL-003: He says he will leave me if I don't sleep with him.

That is pressure, and it is not okay. Your body is your choice. A healthy partner does not use threats. If you feel unsafe or forced, help is available.

*Where to go: Help now if forced; risk: Low; sources: HDI2*

### REL-004: My friends laugh at me because I have never had sex.

Lots of young people wait, and that is a healthy choice. You decide when you are ready, not your friends. Real friends respect your choices.

*risk: None; sources: HDI2*

### REL-005: An older man gives me money and gifts and now wants sex.

Gifts or money never mean you owe anyone sex. Relationships with much older men who give money often come with pressure and risks. If you are under 18, sex with an adult is a crime in Rwanda. You can get confidential help: call 116 or 3029.

**For ages 10-14:** An adult who gives you money or gifts and wants sex with you is doing something very wrong and against the law. It is not your fault. Do not keep it secret: tell a trusted adult or call the free child helpline 116.

*Where to go: 116; Isange 3029; risk: High; sources: HDI2; LAW; RNP; UNHCR*

### REL-006: Is it wrong to ask a girl if she wants to have sex?

Asking is the right thing to do. Asking and then respecting the answer, whether yes or no, is what consent looks like. Never push after a no.

*risk: None; sources: HDI2*

### REL-007: My boyfriend checks my phone and tells me who I can talk to.

Controlling who you see or talk to is a form of abuse in a relationship. You deserve trust and freedom. Talk to someone you trust, and if you feel unsafe, help is available.

*Where to go: GBV line 3512; Isange 3029; risk: High; sources: VAW*

### REL-008: My boyfriend hits me when we argue. Isn't fighting normal for couples?

Disagreements happen, but hitting is never normal or okay. This is violence. You can get confidential help: Isange One Stop Centre 3029 or the GBV line 3512. If you are in danger now, call 112.

*Where to go: Isange One Stop Centre 3029 (toll-free); child helpline 116; GBV line 3512; emergency 112; risk: High; sources: VAW; HDI2; RIB*

### REL-009: How can I talk to my parents about sex and relationships?

Many young people find this hard, and many parents do too. Try a calm, private moment, start with a general question, or talk first to another trusted adult like an aunt. You can also use Intyoza to get your facts first.

*risk: None; sources: HDI1; HDI2*

### REL-010: At what age is it legal to have sex in Rwanda?

Rwandan law punishes adults who have sex with children (defilement) with 20 to 25 years in prison, and life imprisonment when the child is under 14. Sex between children aged 14 or older without violence or threats is not punished. The legal age of marriage is 21.

**For ages 20-24:** Sex between consenting adults is legal. But an adult who has sex with anyone under 18 commits defilement, punished by 20 to 25 years in prison, or life imprisonment if the child is under 14. The legal age of marriage is 21.

*risk: None; sources: LAW; HDI2*

### REL-011: How do I know if my relationship is healthy?

A healthy relationship has respect, trust and honest talk. Both people can say no, make decisions together and spend time with friends and family. Fear, threats or control are warning signs.

*risk: None; sources: HDI2; VAW*

### REL-012: How do I say no to sex without losing my partner?

You can be kind and clear: 'I like you, but I am not ready for sex.' You do not owe anyone an explanation. Suggest other ways to spend time together. A partner who truly cares will respect your no; if they leave because of it, that tells you about them, not about your worth.

*risk: None; sources: HDI2*

### REL-013: How do I know if I am ready for sex?

Being ready means you want it for yourself, not because of pressure, you trust and can talk openly with your partner, you both agree, and you know how to prevent pregnancy and STIs. If you feel unsure, scared or pushed, you are not ready, and waiting is always okay.

**For ages 10-14:** At your age, your body and feelings are still growing, and it is healthiest to wait. Having a crush or wanting to be close to someone is normal. If anyone, especially an older person, wants sex with you, that is not okay: tell a trusted adult or call 116.

*risk: None; sources: HDI2*

### REL-014: Is it safe to send nude photos to my boyfriend or girlfriend?

It is risky. Once sent, you lose control of a photo: it can be shared, kept after a breakup or used to threaten you. Sharing sexual images of anyone under 18 is a crime. You can always say no. If someone threatens to share your photos, it is not your fault: save the messages and report to RIB on 166 or the GBV line 3512.

*Where to go: RIB 166 if threatened; risk: Low; sources: RIB*

### REL-015: Is watching pornography bad for me?

Pornography is not real life: it often shows sex without consent talk or protection and unrealistic bodies. Watching a lot can create pressure and wrong expectations about sex and partners. If it makes you feel bad or you cannot stop, talk to someone you trust or a counsellor.

**For ages 10-14:** Pornography is made for adults and does not show real, respectful relationships. If someone shows you sexual pictures or videos, that is not okay: tell a trusted adult or call 116. If you saw something that upset you, talking about it helps.

*risk: None; sources: HDI2*

### REL-016: We broke up and I feel terrible. How do I cope?

Breakups hurt, and it is normal to feel sad, angry or confused. Talk to friends or family, keep doing things you enjoy, sleep and eat well, and give yourself time. Avoid checking their social media all the time. If sadness lasts weeks or you think of hurting yourself, get help: call 114 or talk to a health worker.

*risk: None; sources: MH*

### REL-017: Is it safe to meet someone I met online?

Be careful: people online are not always who they say. Do not share your address, school or private photos. If you decide to meet, meet in a busy public place during the day, tell a trusted person where you are going, and leave if you feel uncomfortable. Be very cautious of anyone offering money, gifts or jobs.

*risk: None; sources: SAFE*

### REL-018: How do I tell someone I like them?

Be yourself and keep it simple, for example: 'I enjoy spending time with you. Would you like to hang out?' Choose a private, relaxed moment. Be ready for any answer: if they say no, respect it, it takes courage to ask and it says nothing bad about you.

*risk: None; sources: HDI2*

### REL-019: I am attracted to people of the same sex. Is something wrong with me?

Nothing is wrong with you. Many young people have questions about attraction, and feelings can become clearer over time. You deserve respect, safety and good health care like everyone else. Talking to someone you trust or a counsellor can help, and health services, including HIV prevention, are for everyone.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner; counsellor; risk: None; sources: MH*

### REL-020: Is it okay to wait until marriage?

Yes. Choosing to wait is a valid and healthy choice, and it is the surest way to avoid pregnancy and STIs. It is still useful to learn about consent, contraception and STIs, so you are ready to protect yourself whenever you decide.

*risk: None; sources: HDI2*

### REL-021: My friends push me to drink alcohol at parties. What should I do?

You can say no: 'No thanks, I'm fine.' Alcohol makes it harder to make safe choices about sex and consent and raises the risk of violence. Stay with friends you trust, keep your drink with you, and plan how to get home safely.

*risk: Low; sources: HDI2; MH*

### REL-022: Someone I met online asks me for money or private photos.

Be careful: this is a common scam and can lead to blackmail. Do not send money or photos, stop talking to them and block them. If they threaten you, save the messages and report to RIB on 166. It is not your fault.

*Where to go: RIB 166; risk: Low; sources: RIB*

### REL-023: How can I keep my social media accounts safe?

Use strong passwords and don't share them, even with a partner. Turn on two-step verification, make your accounts private, and don't post your address, school or location. Think before posting: once shared, photos can spread.

*risk: None; sources: RIB*

### REL-024: People are bullying me online.

Cyberbullying is not your fault. Don't reply, take screenshots, block and report the accounts on the platform. Tell a trusted adult. If there are threats or sexual images, report to RIB on 166, or 116 if you are under 18. Talk to someone if it is affecting your mood.

*Where to go: RIB 166; 116; risk: Low; sources: RIB; MH*

### REL-025: Someone shared a nude photo of a classmate in our group chat.

Do not share it further and delete it. Sharing sexual images of someone without consent, especially of anyone under 18, is a crime and seriously hurts the person. Tell a trusted adult or report to RIB on 166. Support your classmate rather than joining in.

*Where to go: RIB 166; risk: Low; sources: RIB; LAW*

### REL-026: My partner and I argue a lot. How can we communicate better?

Choose a calm moment, talk about how you feel using 'I' sentences, listen without interrupting, and look for a solution together. Take a break if things get heated. Arguments happen, but insults, threats or violence are never okay.

*risk: None; sources: HDI2*

### REL-027: I think my partner is cheating on me.

That feeling hurts. Try to talk calmly and honestly with your partner about what you have noticed. Whatever happens, protect your health: use condoms, and consider an STI and HIV test. You deserve a relationship built on trust.

*risk: Low; sources: HDI2*

### REL-028: I am 16 and dating a man who is 28.

Big age gaps often mean unequal power, which can lead to pressure, risky sex and less control over your choices. In Rwanda, an adult having sex with someone under 18 is defilement, a serious crime, even if you agree. If you feel pressured or unsafe, call 116 or talk to a trusted adult.

*Where to go: 116 if under 18; risk: Low; sources: HDI2; LAW*

### REL-029: How do I end a relationship safely?

Be clear and calm, choose a public or safe place, and keep it short. If your partner has been violent or controlling, tell a friend or family member first, and consider ending it by message. If you feel unsafe, call the GBV line 3512 or 112.

*Where to go: GBV line 3512 if unsafe; risk: None; sources: VAW*

### REL-030: My parents don't allow me to have a boyfriend or girlfriend.

Parents often worry about your safety and studies. Try to understand their concerns and talk honestly. Even if you disagree, it is important to stay safe: avoid secret meetings in isolated places, and know you can still get health information and services if you need them.

*risk: None; sources: HDI1*

### REL-031: Is kissing safe?

Kissing does not pass HIV or cause pregnancy. It can pass colds and cold sores (herpes on the lips). Only kiss when you both want to, and remember you can always say no.

*risk: None; sources: WHO*

### REL-032: How do I know if it is love or just a crush?

A crush is strong attraction, often to someone you don't know well, and can fade quickly. Love grows over time with trust, respect, care and knowing each other's good and bad sides. Both feelings are normal; take time to get to know the person.

*risk: None; sources: HDI2*

### REL-033: What does equality look like in a relationship?

Both partners have equal say in decisions, including about sex, contraception, money and time with friends. Both share responsibilities and respect each other's choices, studies and goals. Neither controls or threatens the other.

*risk: None; sources: HDI2*

### REL-034: My boyfriend wants us to have sex without a condom to prove trust.

Using a condom is not about trust; it is about protecting both of you, since many STIs have no symptoms. You have the right to insist on a condom or say no to sex. If you both want to stop using condoms, get tested together first and choose another method for pregnancy.

*risk: Low; sources: HDI2*

## GBV and safety

### GBV-001: I was raped last night. What should I do?

What happened is not your fault. Go to the nearest Isange One Stop Centre as soon as you can, or call 3029 (toll-free). They give free medical, psychological and legal help. Medicine to prevent HIV works best within 72 hours, and emergency contraception within 5 days. If you are in danger now, call 112.

**For ages 10-14:** What happened is not your fault. Tell an adult you trust right away, or call the free child helpline 116. Go to Isange One Stop Centre or a health centre as soon as possible: they will take care of you and keep you safe. If you are in danger now, call 112.

*Where to go: Isange One Stop Centre 3029; 112; risk: Emergency; sources: UNHCR; RNP; PEP; EC*

### GBV-002: A relative touches me in ways I don't like and tells me to keep it secret.

This is not okay, and it is not your fault. You do not have to keep this secret. Tell an adult you trust, or call the free child helpline 116. Isange One Stop Centres (3029) can also help you.

*Where to go: 116; Isange 3029; risk: Emergency; sources: RNP; RIB*

### GBV-003: My parents want me to marry a man and I am only 16.

The legal age of marriage in Rwanda is 21, and no one can force you to marry. You can get confidential help from the child helpline 116, the GBV line 3512 or Isange One Stop Centre 3029.

*Where to go: 116; 3512; Isange 3029; risk: High; sources: HDI2; RIB*

### GBV-004: How do I report gender-based violence?

You can call the GBV line 3512, Isange One Stop Centre 3029, or the child helpline 116 if you are under 18. For an emergency, call 112. You can also contact RIB on 166.

*Where to go: Isange One Stop Centre 3029 (toll-free); child helpline 116; GBV line 3512; emergency 112; RIB 166; risk: Low; sources: RIB; RNP*

### GBV-005: If I go to Isange, will they tell the police?

When you report at Isange, the case may be shared with law enforcement. You can ask the staff what will happen before you decide, and they can still give you medical care and support.

*Where to go: Isange One Stop Centre 3029; risk: Low; sources: UNHCR*

### GBV-006: Is it rape if my husband or boyfriend forces me?

Yes. Forcing someone into sex is violence, even by a partner or husband. You deserve safety and help. Call Isange One Stop Centre 3029 or the GBV line 3512.

*Where to go: Isange 3029; 3512; risk: High; sources: VAW; HDI2*

### GBV-007: My friend told me someone is abusing her. How can I help?

Listen without judging and tell her it is not her fault. Share the help contacts, like Isange 3029 or the GBV line 3512, and offer to go with her. Respect her choices, but if she is in danger now, call 112.

*Where to go: Isange 3029; 3512; 112; risk: Low; sources: VAW; RIB*

### GBV-008: Someone is threatening to share my nude photos if I don't do what they say.

This is not your fault, and you do not have to do what they say. Do not send more photos or money. Save the messages as evidence and report it to RIB on 166 or the GBV line 3512. Tell an adult you trust.

*Where to go: RIB 166; 3512; risk: High; sources: RIB*

### GBV-009: Since it happened I can't sleep and I feel scared all the time.

These feelings are a common reaction after violence, and you do not have to face them alone. Counsellors at Isange One Stop Centres and health centres can help you feel safer and cope.

*Where to go: Isange 3029; health centre; risk: High; sources: VAW*

### GBV-010: What counts as gender-based violence?

Gender-based violence includes physical violence like hitting, sexual violence like rape or unwanted touching, emotional abuse like threats and insults, and economic abuse like taking someone's money or stopping them from working. It is a crime in Rwanda, and help is free: GBV line 3512, Isange 3029, child helpline 116.

*Where to go: GBV line 3512; Isange 3029; risk: Low; sources: VAW; LAW*

### GBV-011: What should I do to keep evidence after sexual assault?

Your safety and health come first. Go to an Isange One Stop Centre or health facility as soon as possible, ideally within 72 hours. If you can, avoid bathing or changing clothes before, and bring the clothes you wore in a paper or cloth bag. Even if you have washed, still go: you can get medicine to prevent HIV and pregnancy, and free support.

*Where to go: Isange One Stop Centre 3029; 112; risk: Emergency; sources: ISG; PEP; EC*

### GBV-012: A teacher or boss keeps touching me and making sexual comments.

That is sexual harassment and it is not your fault. You have the right to be safe at school and work. Write down what happened and when, tell a trusted adult or the school leadership, and you can report it to RIB on 166, the GBV line 3512 or Isange 3029. If you are under 18, call 116.

*Where to go: GBV line 3512; Isange 3029; 116; risk: High; sources: VAW; LAW*

### GBV-013: Someone is harassing me online with sexual messages.

This is not your fault and you do not have to reply. Block them, take screenshots of the messages, and report the account on the platform. Tell a trusted adult and report it to RIB on 166 or the GBV line 3512, especially if there are threats or they ask for photos.

*Where to go: RIB 166; GBV line 3512; risk: High; sources: RIB*

### GBV-014: Can boys be victims of sexual abuse too?

Yes. Boys and men can also be sexually abused, and it is never their fault. It does not make anyone less of a man. Help is the same and free: Isange One Stop Centre 3029, child helpline 116 for under 18, or RIB 166.

*Where to go: Isange 3029; 116; risk: Low; sources: VAW*

### GBV-015: Someone offered me a job abroad and wants my passport. Is it safe?

Be very careful: fake job offers are a common way people are trafficked. Do not give your passport or ID to anyone, check the company with officials, and talk to your family before accepting. If you suspect trafficking or someone is being held, call RIB on 166 or 112.

*Where to go: RIB 166; 116; 3512; risk: High; sources: RIB*

### GBV-016: I have sex with someone for money to pay for my needs.

Thank you for sharing this. Many young people face pressure to exchange sex for money or things they need, and you deserve to be safe. These relationships often bring violence, HIV and pregnancy risks. Use condoms every time, ask about PrEP, and know you can get confidential support: child helpline 116 if under 18, Isange 3029, or a youth corner.

*Where to go: 116; Isange 3029; youth-friendly corner; risk: High; sources: HDI2; VAW*

### GBV-017: What services does Isange One Stop Centre give?

Isange One Stop Centres give free, confidential care to people affected by gender-based violence and child abuse: medical care including medicine to prevent HIV and pregnancy, counselling, police and legal support, and a safe place in some cases. There are 48 centres linked to district and teaching hospitals. Call 3029 toll-free.

*Where to go: Isange One Stop Centre 3029; risk: None; sources: ISG*

### GBV-018: My parent or guardian hurts me or touches me in a bad way.

This is abuse and it is not your fault. You deserve to be safe at home. Tell a teacher, relative or another adult you trust, or call the free child helpline 116. If you are in danger now, call 112. Isange One Stop Centres (3029) help children too.

*Where to go: 116; Isange 3029; 112; risk: Emergency; sources: RIB; LAW*

### GBV-019: Is it abuse if my partner insults me and makes me feel worthless?

Yes. Constant insults, humiliation, threats and making you feel worthless are emotional abuse, which is a form of violence. It is not your fault and you deserve respect. Talk to someone you trust, and you can get confidential help from the GBV line 3512 or Isange 3029.

*Where to go: GBV line 3512; Isange 3029; risk: Low; sources: VAW*

### GBV-020: My family wants me to marry the man who got me pregnant.

No one can force you to marry, and the legal age of marriage in Rwanda is 21. Being pregnant does not mean you must marry. If the man is an adult and you are under 18, it is a crime. You can get confidential help from 116, the GBV line 3512 or Isange 3029.

*Where to go: 116; GBV line 3512; risk: High; sources: LAW*

### GBV-021: Someone keeps following me and watching me everywhere.

Being followed and watched is stalking, and it can be dangerous. Tell people you trust, avoid walking alone, keep records of when and where it happens, and report it to RIB on 166 or the GBV line 3512. If you are in danger now, call 112.

*Where to go: RIB 166; GBV line 3512; 112; risk: High; sources: RIB*

### GBV-022: Is it rape if I was drunk or asleep?

Yes. A person who is asleep, unconscious or too drunk to decide cannot consent, so sex in that situation is rape. It is not your fault, whatever you drank. Go to Isange One Stop Centre (3029) as soon as possible, ideally within 72 hours, for free medical care and support.

*Where to go: Isange One Stop Centre 3029; risk: High; sources: LAW; VAW*

### GBV-023: I was abused as a child and I still think about it a lot.

Thank you for sharing this. What happened was not your fault, and it is common for memories to come back years later. Counselling can really help. You can talk to a counsellor at a health centre, Isange One Stop Centre (3029), or call the free line 114.

*Where to go: Isange 3029; health centre; 114; risk: Low; sources: VAW; MH*

### GBV-024: Can I get free legal help after violence?

Yes. Every district has an access to justice office (MAJ) that gives free legal advice. Isange One Stop Centres also connect survivors to police and legal support. You can ask them what your options are before deciding.

*Where to go: District MAJ office; Isange 3029; risk: None; sources: LAW*

## Emotional wellbeing

### EMO-001: I feel sad most of the time and don't enjoy anything anymore.

You are not alone. About 1 in 7 young people has a mental health condition like depression or anxiety, and help works. Talk to someone you trust, a health worker, or call the free RBC health line 114. If you ever think about hurting yourself, get help right away.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: MH*

### EMO-002: I don't want to live anymore.

I am really glad you told me. You matter, and you do not have to go through this alone. Please call 112 now, or the free RBC health line 114, or go to the nearest health centre or hospital, and tell someone you trust to stay with you.

*Where to go: 112; nearest health centre or hospital; risk: Emergency; sources: MH*

### EMO-003: I feel stressed about school and relationships. What can I do?

Regular sleep, exercise and talking with people you trust help a lot. Breaking problems into small steps can make them easier. If stress stops you from daily life, a health worker can help.

*risk: None; sources: MH*

### EMO-004: I hurt myself when I feel bad.

Thank you for telling me. You deserve care and support. Please talk to a health worker or someone you trust soon. If you are hurt or in danger right now, call 112 or go to the nearest health centre.

*Where to go: 112; health centre; risk: Emergency; sources: MH*

### EMO-005: I drink a lot to forget my problems.

Using alcohol to cope can make problems worse and is a risk to your health. Talking to someone you trust or a health worker can help you find better ways to cope.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: MH*

### EMO-006: I am a young mother and I feel overwhelmed.

Being a young parent is hard, and feeling overwhelmed is common. You deserve support. Talk to a health worker at your clinic visits, and connect with other young parents in Community.

*Where to go: Health centre; Community group; risk: Low; sources: MH*

### EMO-007: Kids at school make fun of my body.

Being bullied is not your fault, and it can really hurt. Tell a teacher, parent or adult you trust. Every body changes at its own pace in puberty.

*risk: Low; sources: MH; PUB*

### EMO-008: Sometimes my heart races and I can't breathe and I feel I will die. What is happening?

That sounds like a panic attack, which is frightening but usually passes within minutes and is not dangerous. Breathe slowly: in for 4 counts, hold, out for 6. Name 5 things you can see around you. If it happens often, talk to a health worker or call 114. If you have chest pain or it does not stop, get medical help.

*Where to go: 114; health centre; risk: Low; sources: MH; HL*

### EMO-009: I feel lonely and have no friends.

Feeling lonely is painful and more common than people show. Try small steps: join a club, sport, church or youth group, or talk to one classmate about something you both like. Real friendships take time. If loneliness makes you very sad, talk to a counsellor or call 114.

*risk: Low; sources: MH*

### EMO-010: I hate how I look.

Many young people feel this way, especially while their bodies change. Your worth is not your looks. Try to limit comparing yourself to filtered photos online, notice what your body can do, and spend time with people who make you feel good. If these thoughts take over or affect eating, talk to a health worker.

*risk: Low; sources: MH; PUB*

### EMO-011: During commemoration (Kwibuka) I feel very sad and have flashbacks.

Many people feel strong grief, fear or flashbacks during Kwibuka, and you are not alone. Stay with people you trust, rest, and talk about how you feel. Counsellors are available during commemoration, and you can call the RBC health line 114 for free support.

*Where to go: 114; 7494; risk: Low; sources: HL*

### EMO-012: My friend says she wants to kill herself. What should I do?

Take it seriously. Stay with her, listen without judging, and tell her you care. Do not keep it secret: tell a trusted adult now. If she is in immediate danger, call 112 or take her to the nearest health centre or hospital. You can also call 114 for advice.

*Where to go: 112; 114; health centre; risk: High; sources: MH*

### EMO-013: Where can I talk to a counsellor?

You can talk to a counsellor at your health centre, a school counsellor or patron, or call the RBC health line 114 for free. Isange One Stop Centres (3029) have counsellors for people affected by violence. Talking to someone is a sign of strength.

*Where to go: 114; health centre; risk: None; sources: HL; MH*

### EMO-014: I can't sleep at night because I think too much.

Try a routine: go to bed and wake up at the same time, put your phone away an hour before sleep, avoid coffee and energy drinks late, and write down your worries before bed. If poor sleep lasts weeks or comes with sadness, talk to a health worker.

*risk: None; sources: MH*

### EMO-015: I haven't been feeling well for some days.

I'm sorry you're not feeling well. It helps to know more: is it your body, like pain, fever, bleeding or discharge, or your feelings, like sadness, stress or worry? If you have fever, strong pain or bleeding, please go to a health centre. You can also talk to a health worker or call the free line 114.

*Where to go: Health centre; 114; risk: Low; sources: MH*

### EMO-016: I don't want to call anyone or get help.

I hear you, and it's okay if you don't want to call right now. I'm still here. Is there someone you trust nearby, a friend or family member, you could tell that you're not okay and ask to stay with you? Writing down what you feel can also help. If you feel you might hurt yourself now, please call 112 or go to the nearest health centre.

*Where to go: 114; 112; risk: High; sources: MH*

### EMO-017: I feel the urge to hurt myself. What can I do right now?

Thank you for reaching out. Right now, move to a safe place near other people, put away anything you could hurt yourself with, and contact someone you trust or call the free line 114. Breathe slowly and remind yourself the urge will pass. If you are in danger, call 112.

*Where to go: 114; 112; risk: High; sources: MH*

### EMO-018: My father died and I can't stop feeling sad.

I'm very sorry. Grief can bring sadness, anger, guilt and tiredness, and it comes in waves. Let yourself feel it, talk about the person with people you trust, and keep a daily routine. If the sadness doesn't ease over time or stops you from living, talk to a counsellor or call 114.

*Where to go: 114; health centre; risk: Low; sources: MH*

### EMO-019: I get angry very quickly and sometimes hit things.

Anger is a normal feeling, but acting on it can hurt you and others. When you feel it rising, step away, breathe slowly and count to ten, or go for a walk. Exercise and talking about what makes you angry help. If anger often gets out of control, talk to a counsellor.

*risk: Low; sources: MH*

### EMO-020: My family expects too much from me and I feel pressure all the time.

Family expectations can feel heavy. Try to share honestly how you feel with a parent or trusted relative, and focus on doing your best rather than being perfect. Make time for rest and things you enjoy. If the pressure makes you very anxious or sad, talk to a counsellor.

*risk: Low; sources: MH*

### EMO-021: Social media makes me feel bad about myself.

Many posts show filtered, edited moments, not real life. If you feel worse after scrolling, try muting or unfollowing accounts that make you compare yourself, set time limits, and spend more time on offline activities and with people who support you.

*risk: None; sources: MH*

### EMO-022: I feel that my body does not match who I am inside.

Thank you for sharing something so personal. Questions about gender identity happen to some young people, and your feelings deserve respect. Talking with a counsellor or health worker you trust can help you explore them safely. You deserve safety and good care.

*Where to go: Counsellor; youth-friendly corner; risk: None; sources: MH*

### EMO-023: I have stopped eating because I want to be thinner.

Thank you for telling me. Not eating can seriously harm your body and mind, and these feelings are hard to handle alone. Please talk to a health worker soon, or call the free line 114. Telling someone you trust can also help. You deserve support.

*Where to go: Health centre; 114; risk: High; sources: MH*

### EMO-024: My friend seems sad all the time. How can I help?

Ask gently how they are and listen without judging. Let them know you care and spend time together. Encourage them to talk to a counsellor or call 114, and offer to go with them. If they talk about suicide or self-harm, tell a trusted adult right away.

*risk: None; sources: MH*

## Myths and misconceptions

### MYT-001: Does having sex cure pimples?

Myth. Pimples are common in puberty because of hormones. Sex does not cure them. A health worker can help if acne is severe.

*risk: None; sources: PUB; HDI1; HDI2*

### MYT-002: Does sex make breasts or buttocks grow bigger?

Myth. Breasts and body shape change because of puberty hormones and genes, not sex.

*risk: None; sources: PUB; HDI1*

### MYT-003: Does having sex stop period pain?

Myth. Sex is not a treatment for period pain. If pain stops you from daily activities, see a health worker.

*risk: None; sources: MEN; HDI1*

### MYT-004: Does a girl grow faster after her first time having sex?

Myth. Growth happens because of puberty and has its own timing. Sex does not change it.

*risk: None; sources: PUB; HDI1*

### MYT-005: You can't get pregnant the first time.

Myth. Pregnancy can happen any time there is unprotected sex, including the first time.

*risk: None; sources: PUB; HDI1*

### MYT-006: Family planning makes you unable to have children.

Myth. Modern methods do not cause infertility.

*risk: None; sources: FP; HDI2*

### MYT-007: Condoms have tiny holes that HIV passes through.

Myth. Condoms do not have holes that HIV can pass through, and they are not laced with HIV.

*risk: None; sources: FPHM*

### MYT-008: Condoms make men weak or unable to have children.

Myth. Condoms do not make men sterile, weak or impotent, and they do not lower sex drive.

*risk: None; sources: FPHM*

### MYT-009: A condom can get lost inside a woman's body.

Myth. A condom cannot get lost in a woman's body. If it slips off inside, it can be removed, and emergency contraception can help prevent pregnancy.

*risk: None; sources: FPHM; FPH*

### MYT-010: Condoms are only for sex workers or people who cheat.

Myth. Married couples and many other people use condoms. Using one shows you care about your health.

*risk: None; sources: FPHM; HDI1*

### MYT-011: Condoms take away all the pleasure.

Partly true for some people. Some men feel less sensation, but a drop or two of water-based lubricant inside the condom can help. Talking with your partner also helps.

*risk: None; sources: FPH; HDI1*

### MYT-012: HIV can be cured.

Myth. HIV cannot be cured, but treatment controls it so people can live long, healthy lives.

*risk: None; sources: STI; HDI1*

### MYT-013: Washing after sex prevents pregnancy and STIs.

Myth. Washing or douching does not prevent pregnancy or STIs, and douching can increase the risk of infection.

*risk: None; sources: FPH*

### MYT-014: Bathing during your period makes you infertile.

Myth. Washing during your period is healthy and does not cause infertility.

*risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MYT-015: With abortion you either die or go to jail.

Not true as stated. Unsafe abortion done by untrained people is very dangerous. In Rwanda, abortion done by a recognised doctor is legal for some reasons, such as when the pregnant person is a child or after rape.

*Where to go: Recognised doctor at a licensed hospital; risk: Low; sources: AP; LAW; HDI2*

### MYT-016: The hymen shows whether a girl is a virgin.

Myth. The hymen is a thin, stretchy tissue that varies from girl to girl. It can stretch from sport or using tampons, and many girls do not bleed the first time they have sex. No test can prove virginity, and so-called virginity tests are harmful.

*risk: None; sources: WHO*

### MYT-017: Masturbation causes infertility or madness.

Myth. Masturbation does not cause infertility, madness, weakness, blindness or small genitals. It is a normal behaviour.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### MYT-018: Menstrual blood is dirty or can be used for witchcraft.

Myth. Menstrual blood is normal blood and tissue from the uterus. It is not dirty, dangerous or magical. Girls should never be ashamed of their periods.

*risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MYT-019: Eating certain foods or drinking something prevents pregnancy.

Myth. No food, drink, herb or soda prevents pregnancy. Only modern methods like condoms, pills, injections, implants, IUDs and emergency pills work.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### MYT-020: Only people with many partners get STIs.

Myth. Anyone who has sex can get an STI, even with one partner, because many infections have no symptoms. Condoms and testing protect everyone.

*risk: None; sources: STI*

### MYT-021: Circumcised men don't need condoms.

Myth. Circumcision lowers HIV risk but does not prevent it fully, and it does not prevent pregnancy or most other STIs. Condoms are still needed.

*risk: None; sources: WHO*

### MYT-022: Sex during pregnancy harms the baby.

Mostly a myth. In a normal pregnancy, sex does not harm the baby, which is protected inside the womb. A health worker may advise avoiding sex if there is bleeding, leaking fluid or other complications. Condoms still protect against STIs.

*Where to go: Health centre (antenatal care); risk: None; sources: AP*

### MYT-023: The implant can move around the body or get lost.

Myth. The implant stays where it is placed under the skin of the arm. In very rare cases it moves slightly, and a health worker can find and remove it.

*risk: None; sources: FPH*

### MYT-024: Contraceptives cause birth defects or cancer.

Myth. Modern contraceptives do not cause birth defects, and they do not cause cancer; some even lower the risk of certain cancers. They are among the most studied medicines in the world.

*risk: None; sources: FP*

### MYT-025: Boys get sick if they don't have sex.

Myth. Not having sex does not harm boys or men. Sperm that isn't released is simply reabsorbed by the body or released during wet dreams.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### MYT-026: You can tell if someone has an STI by looking or smelling.

Myth. Most STIs have no visible signs or smell. The only way to know is to get tested, and both partners should test.

*risk: None; sources: STI*

### MYT-027: Taking two emergency pills works better.

Myth. Taking extra emergency pills does not give more protection and can cause more side effects. Take the dose as directed, as soon as possible after unprotected sex.

*risk: None; sources: EC*

### MYT-028: The implant can paralyse your arm.

Myth. The implant is placed just under the skin and does not affect nerves, muscles or arm movement. Some people have slight bruising or soreness for a few days after insertion.

*risk: None; sources: FPH*

### MYT-029: Cold drinks or cold water during your period cause infertility.

Myth. Cold drinks and water do not affect your period or fertility. Drink what is comfortable for you.

*risk: None; sources: MEN*

### MYT-030: You can't get pregnant if you have sex standing up.

Myth. Pregnancy can happen in any position. Sperm moves on its own, whatever the position.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### MYT-031: Traditional medicine can make someone love you.

Myth. No medicine, potion or charm can make someone love you. Love grows from respect, trust and choice. Some of these products can also be harmful to your health.

*risk: None; sources: TBC*

### MYT-032: Sex makes boys grow taller and stronger.

Myth. Height and strength come from genes, puberty, good food, sleep and exercise, not from sex.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

## Rights and services

### SRV-014: What is sexual and reproductive health?

Sexual and reproductive health means being well in body, mind and relationships when it comes to sex, growing up and having children. It includes understanding your body, puberty and periods, making safe choices, preventing unplanned pregnancy, HIV and other STIs, respectful relationships and consent, and being free from violence. In Rwanda, young people have the right to accurate information and to confidential, youth-friendly services at health centres and youth corners.

*risk: None; sources: AP; LAW25*

### SRV-001: What does Rwanda's new health law mean for young people?

Law N° 026/2025 regulating healthcare services, in force since 18 September 2025, lowers the age to consent to health services, including sexual and reproductive health, from 18 to 15. This means people aged 15 and above can get services like contraception and HIV prevention without parental or third-party consent.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: LAW25*

### SRV-002: Will the health worker tell my parents or school?

Health workers must keep your health information confidential. If you are 15 or older, you can get sexual and reproductive health services on your own. The exception is when you are in danger, for example abuse, where they may involve protection services to keep you safe. You can ask the health worker what will be shared before you talk.

*risk: None; sources: LAW25*

### SRV-003: What is a youth corner?

A youth corner, or youth-friendly service, is a space at many health centres and youth centres where young people can get information and services such as counselling, contraception, condoms, HIV testing and STI care, from staff trained to welcome youth without judgement.

*Where to go: Youth-friendly corner at health centre; risk: None; sources: TBC*

### SRV-004: What helpline numbers can I call in Rwanda?

Emergency (police, danger): 112. Health call centre, including mental health support: 114, toll-free. Isange One Stop Centre for violence: 3029, toll-free. GBV line: 3512. Child helpline for under 18: 116, toll-free. RIB: 166.

*risk: None; sources: HL*

### SRV-005: A health worker judged me when I asked for condoms. What can I do?

You have the right to respectful and confidential care. That experience was not okay. You can ask to see another provider, go to a youth corner, or use a pharmacy. You can also tell the health centre's management about what happened.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: TBC*

### SRV-006: Do young people with disabilities have the same sexual health rights?

Yes. Young people with disabilities have the same rights to information, relationships, contraception, HIV care and protection from violence. Health services should be accessible, including communication support. If services are not accessible, ask for help or report it to the health facility.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: VAW*

### SRV-007: Does community health insurance (Mutuelle) cover sexual health services?

Community-based health insurance (Mutuelle de santé) covers many services at public health centres and hospitals. Many family planning, HIV and antenatal services are also offered free at public facilities. Ask at your health centre what is covered for you.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: TBC*

### SRV-008: Can a community health worker give me family planning?

Yes. In Rwanda, community health workers in your village can provide some methods, such as pills, injections and condoms, and advice, and refer you to the health centre for others like the implant or IUD.

*Where to go: Community health worker; risk: None; sources: FP*

### SRV-009: Can someone force me to take an HIV test?

No. HIV testing should be voluntary and done with your consent, along with counselling, and your result is confidential. If you are 15 or older, you can consent to testing yourself.

*risk: None; sources: LAW25; WHO*

### SRV-010: What does Intyoza do with what I write?

Intyoza uses your message only to find the best answer. It does not need your name, and it does not share your conversation. If you turn on memory, only the topics you asked about are kept on your phone, not your messages.

*risk: None; sources: MH*

### SRV-011: Where can I get help at night after violence?

Isange One Stop Centres are linked to hospitals; if the Isange office is closed at night, go to the hospital's emergency department, where staff can give urgent care including medicine to prevent HIV and pregnancy. You can also call 112.

*Where to go: Hospital emergency department; 112; risk: None; sources: ISG*

### SRV-012: Where can I learn more about sexual health at school?

Rwanda's school curriculum includes comprehensive sexuality education in science and social studies, covering growth, relationships, rights and HIV prevention. You can also ask your school nurse, patron or matron, or a youth corner at a health centre.

*risk: None; sources: TBC*

### SRV-013: I am under 15. Can I still get help at a health centre?

Yes. Health centres help children too. For some services, a health worker may involve a parent or trusted adult, but if you are in danger or being abused, they will help you and protect you. You can also call the free child helpline 116.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: LAW25*

## Boys' health

### BOY-001: I get erections at random times, even in class. Is that normal?

Yes. During puberty, hormones cause erections often and sometimes for no reason, even when you are not thinking about sex. It is normal and usually goes away in a few minutes. Thinking about something else, or sitting with a book on your lap, helps.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### BOY-002: I have sudden, very strong pain in my testicle.

Sudden severe pain in a testicle can be a twisted testicle, which needs surgery within hours to save it. Go to the nearest hospital or health centre immediately, or call 112. Do not wait to see if it goes away.

*Where to go: Hospital now; 112; risk: Emergency; sources: WHO*

### BOY-003: One of my testicles is bigger or hangs lower. Is that normal?

Yes. It is very common for one testicle to be slightly bigger or to hang lower than the other. See a health worker if you notice a new lump, swelling, heaviness or pain.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### BOY-004: I come too quickly during sex. What can I do?

Coming quickly is common, especially for young men and in new relationships, and often improves with time and less anxiety. Slowing down, relaxing and talking with your partner help. If it continues to worry you, a health worker can advise privately.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: WHO*

### BOY-005: Sometimes I can't get or keep an erection. Is something wrong?

This happens to most men at some point and is often caused by stress, nerves, tiredness, alcohol or drugs. If it happens often, a health worker can check for other causes. Avoid buying pills from unofficial sellers, as they can be dangerous.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: WHO*

### BOY-006: There is pus or discharge coming from my penis.

Discharge from the penis, often with burning when you pee, is usually a sign of an STI such as gonorrhoea or chlamydia. Go to a health centre for testing and treatment, avoid sex until treated, and your partner should be treated too.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: Low; sources: STI*

### BOY-007: How do I keep my penis clean if I am not circumcised?

Wash daily with clean water. Gently pull back the foreskin as far as it goes comfortably, wash underneath, rinse and pull it back down. Never force it. If it is tight, painful or swollen, see a health worker.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

### BOY-008: Is it okay for boys to cry or talk about their feelings?

Yes. Boys and men have feelings too, and talking about them is a sign of strength, not weakness. Keeping everything inside can lead to stress, anger or sadness. Talk to a friend, family member, teacher or counsellor, or call the free line 114.

*risk: None; sources: MH*

### BOY-009: How do I check my testicles?

Once a month, after a warm bath, gently roll each testicle between your fingers. Get to know how they normally feel. If you find a hard lump, swelling or a change in size, see a health worker. Most lumps are not cancer, but it is best to check early.

*Where to go: Health centre; risk: None; sources: WHO*

### BOY-010: Is it normal for semen to look different sometimes?

Yes. Semen is usually white or greyish and can be thicker or thinner from day to day. See a health worker if it is red or brown, smells bad, or comes with pain, as that can be a sign of infection.

*risk: None; sources: PUB*

## Alcohol and drugs

### DRG-001: How does alcohol affect decisions about sex?

Alcohol makes it harder to think clearly, so people are more likely to have sex they didn't plan, skip condoms, or be pressured or forced. A person who is very drunk cannot give consent. If you drink, stay with friends you trust and plan how to get home safely.

*risk: None; sources: MH; RBCD*

### DRG-002: Is cannabis (marijuana) legal in Rwanda?

No. Using, buying, selling or growing cannabis for personal use is illegal in Rwanda and can lead to arrest. Cannabis can also harm memory, learning and mental health, especially in young people whose brains are still developing.

*risk: None; sources: LAW*

### DRG-003: What does smoking cannabis do to young people?

Cannabis can affect memory, concentration and school performance, and can trigger anxiety, paranoia or serious mental illness in some young people. Regular use can lead to dependence. If you want to stop or reduce, a health worker or the free line 114 can help.

*Where to go: Health centre; 114; risk: Low; sources: RBCD; MH*

### DRG-004: My friend drank too much and won't wake up.

This could be alcohol poisoning, which can kill. Call 112 now. Do not leave them alone. Turn them on their side so they don't choke if they vomit, keep them warm, and do not give them more alcohol, coffee or a cold shower.

*Where to go: 112; health centre; risk: Emergency; sources: WHO*

### DRG-005: I want to stop drinking but I can't.

Wanting to stop is an important first step. Try setting small goals, avoiding places and friends linked to drinking, and telling someone you trust. Health centres in Rwanda treat alcohol problems, and you can call the free line 114 for support. You don't have to do it alone.

*Where to go: Health centre; 114; risk: Low; sources: RBCD*

### DRG-006: How can I protect myself from drinks being spiked?

Keep your drink with you, accept drinks only from people you trust or that you see opened, and don't leave a drink unattended. Stay with friends. If you suddenly feel very drunk, confused or sleepy, tell a friend immediately and get to a safe place. If you think you were assaulted, go to Isange (3029) as soon as possible.

*Where to go: Isange 3029 if assaulted; risk: Low; sources: VAW*

### DRG-007: Is smoking cigarettes, vaping or shisha harmful?

Yes. Tobacco and nicotine are addictive and harm the lungs, heart and growth, and smoking increases the risk of cancer. Vapes also contain nicotine and chemicals. Shisha is banned in Rwanda. The earlier you start, the harder it is to stop.

*risk: None; sources: WHO*

### DRG-008: Where can I get help for drug addiction in Rwanda?

Start at your health centre: mental health services there can assess and treat drug and alcohol problems, and refer you if needed. You can also call the free RBC line 114. Rehabilitation centres provide counselling and psychological treatment. Asking for help is a strong step.

*Where to go: Health centre; 114; risk: Low; sources: RBCD*
